# insurance_claims_tableau_analysis

Data Description

I am using an insurance dataset from Kaggle which is using partially real data points and partially synthetic data points. This table’s rows each represent a policyholder. The table has the following columns: Age, Is_Senior, Marital_Status, Married_Premium_Discount, Prior_Insurance, Prior_Insurance_Premium_Adjustment, Claims_Frequency, Claims_Severity, Claims_Adjustment, Policy_Type, Policy_Adjustment, Premium_Amount, Safe_Driver_Discount, Multi_Policy_Discount, Bundling_Discount, Total_Discounts, Source_of_Lead, Time_Since_First_Contact, Conversion_Status, Website_Visits, Inquiries, Quotes_Requested, Time_to_Conversion, Credit_Score, Premium_Adjustment_Credit, Region, and Premium_Adjustment_Region.

Problem Description

I’m trying to identify what predictive factors out of the given columns can be used as predictive attributes of how much a prospective customer will need to be paid in claims per year.

Method

The first thing I did was use the two claims columns, Claims_Frequency and Claims_Severity, to create a total Claims amount paid per year column. The Claims_Severity column is a text column with values high, medium, and low, which means the severity of the policyholder’s average claim. The Claims_Frequency is just an integer rating from 1 to 5. I made a new column, Claims Frequency Per Year, by converting these numbers to 0 claims per year for rating 1, 1 for rating 2, 2 for rating 3, 3 for rating 4, and 5 for rating 5. I put 5 for 5 because I’m assuming rating 5 to represent 4 or more per year, so 5 would be an average. I made a new integer column, Claims Severity Numeric, that replaces the severity column with an assigned integer for each value. I replaced low with 1500, medium with 5500, and high with 40000. These are just approximations attempting to be realistic averages for each severity category, as the dataset did not specify what each category included in their ranges. The new target column Claims Amount Paid is the product of the frequency and severity columns I created. This represents the annual claims expense of each policyholder. I will then do data analysis trying to identify predictive factors of this new column. I will use descriptive statistics as well as linear regression.

In my analyses, I filter out points with a count of less than 20. 

Limitations of this project

This dataset does not have car information of each policyholder. This is a big missing variable that would be vital in a real world analysis, because the cost of the car directly affects the expected expenses in claims of the client, independent of the client itself. I will try to account for this in my findings to try to find predictive factors that are independent of car cost by using logic and other analysis when possible, however for the most part this would be something that needs to be filtered for in the real world.

Findings

First, I note that the average claims amount paid out of all policyholders in this dataset is $653 per year. This is a useful reference. Also, the vast majority of 90.91% of the entire dataset of policyholders has 0 paid in claims. This is the nature of the car insurance business. The few expensive policyholders bring up the claims expense average a lot. I will recommend certain increases or decreases in premiums for certain categories of policyholders, however this is just for the sake of this project. In reality, we would need to look at a few more years of claims data. I’m assuming this dataset is only for one year. Also, everything we find would need to be filtered by car price, since that is definitely a major influence on claims expenses.

Age shows a positive correlation with average claims amount paid from 18 to 40. It then shows a negative, linear correlation from 40 to 75. The positive correlation is most likely due to people getting increasingly better financial situations with age and thus buying more expensive cars. Unfortunately, without car data, there is no way to isolate age to see if age has an independent effect on claims amount paid for this age range. Also, this positive correlation is not statistically significant because the p value is around .18. For ages 40 to 75, there is a linear regression line of best fit with a slope of -12.8, meaning on average for each year increase in age the average annual claims amount paid decreases by $12.80. The p value however is .07. So there is a 7% chance this correlation is due to chance. I don’t want to remove the outlier that is very high at age 56. The reason is it is very important to factor in these individuals who end up being very expensive in claims, because they are very impactful to the business. I believe the best approach here is to include them, so that the coefficient is reduced in magnitude, and to allow our tolerance for the p value to be a bit higher, like say p < .1, which ours is. So this linear regression -12.80 coefficient is acceptable as statistically significant. There is another limitation to our dataset and this finding which is that we don’t have the amount of driving each policyholder does. Perhaps these older people drive less, which could contribute to why their claims expenses are decreasing. However, I believe it is reasonable to believe that the decrease in claims expenses is at least in part caused by age, due to maturity which manifests as more responsible, slower driving. Also, it is worth mentioning that the R squared here is .09, which is traditionally very low. I believe that this should not be a disqualifying factor because the nature of car insurance claims is that the results are largely affected by the few policyholders who actually have nonzero claims and especially by those who have large expenses in claims. R squared means what percentage of the variance in the dependent variable is explained by the variance in the independent variable. Normally, if the R squared is this low it is reason enough to say that this linear regression line is not valid because it has terrible fit, but as I explained I think we must consider this line due to the nature of auto claims. Ultimately, this would need to be analyzed by filtering car price.

Region appears to have predictive value. There are three categories for region: rural, suburban, and urban. There are thousands of datapoints for each category, so the averages should be reliable. The average claims amount paid for rural is 704.28. The average for suburban is 649.52. The average for urban is 633.71. So rural policyholders may need to pay a little more in premiums. Suburban is right around the average of all policyholders. Urban is slightly lower and could receive a tiny discount.

Marital status appears to have predictive value. There are four categories: divorced, married, single, and widowed. Married and single are around average at 655.10 and 643.80 per year, respectively. Widowed is 547.70, which could qualify for a discount since it is over 15% below average in claims expenses. Divorced is the highest at 779.90. This category could qualify for an extra high premium due to it being nearly 20% above average in claims expenses. I looked at if it would be useful to filter certain values for this like age and prior insurance but did not find anything meaningful. I noticed that married and single had a very large percentage of its group comprised of 18 year olds, but did not really find a reason to justify filtering out 18 year olds because age from 18-40 did not really have any statistically significant relationship with claims expenses.

There doesn’t appear to be any statistically significant relationship between credit score and claims. Visually, I can see some subtle positive correlation from 630 to 730 and a negative from 730 to 780, but the p value of the linear regression lines are way too high for the correlation to be statistically meaningful.

I found that drivers with the safe driver discount had a significantly lower average annual claims expense than those without it. I filtered to only include drivers who had a prior insurance history of over 5 years since you need 5 years of good driving history to get the safe driver discount. The average for those with the discount was $587.5, while the average for those without it was $688. This is a nearly 15% decrease in expenses for those with the safe driver discount, which justifies the discount.

I also found differences in claims expenses for how many years a driver has had insurance, however, since the categories are only <1 year, 1-5 years, and >5 years, I believe this is likely heavily skewed by changing financial situations and thus car prices.

Conclusion

These findings are of course missing the car price data, which is a huge missing piece. In the real world, I would need to filter by car price for each of these analyses. There were not many opportunities to filter by anything in this dataset because there were few variables I found to even be statistically significant in claims expenses, and the few times when I could, there were just not enough datapoints to be resistant to outliers.
